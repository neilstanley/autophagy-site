Of the five practices this app is built around, the sauna has some of the strongest long-term human evidence behind it. Not mechanistic hand-waving, but large cohorts followed for decades. Here is what that evidence actually says, and what it does not.

## What the human data shows

The standout work comes from the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease study in Finland, which followed more than 2,300 middle-aged men for around 20 years. Men who used a sauna four to seven times a week had roughly 40 per cent lower all-cause mortality than those who went once a week, along with markedly lower rates of fatal cardiovascular disease and sudden cardiac death ([the cohort findings are summarised here](https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/s0025-6196(18)30275-1/fulltext)). The relationship was dose-dependent: more frequent use, and longer sessions past about 19 minutes, tracked with better outcomes.

A 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review pulled the wider evidence together and concluded that regular sauna bathing is associated with lower risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and possibly better outcomes for blood pressure, dementia and respiratory conditions. More recently, a randomised controlled trial in people with coronary artery disease found that regular sauna sessions improved vascular function, which starts to move the story from association towards cause.

## Why it plausibly works

A sauna is mild, controlled heat stress. Your heart rate climbs to a level comparable with low-to-moderate exercise, your blood vessels dilate, and over weeks your blood pressure tends to fall. At the cellular level, heat switches on heat shock proteins, which help refold and clear damaged proteins, and it engages the same housekeeping machinery, including autophagy, that this whole approach is built around. Most of that cellular detail is still worked out in the lab rather than in people, so I would lean on the population data as the stronger evidence and treat the mechanism as the likely reason.

## The honest caveats

The headline mortality figures come from observational cohorts, which show association, not proof of cause. People who sauna four times a week in Finland may differ from those who do not in ways that also affect health. The randomised vascular data helps, but we do not have a decades-long trial and probably never will. The strongest evidence is also in Finnish men using traditional hot saunas, so be careful transferring it wholesale to infrared cabins or very different populations. None of this makes the sauna a treatment for any disease. It is a low-risk habit with unusually good supporting data, which is a rare and useful thing.

## How to use it

Aim for traditional sauna heat, around 15 to 20 minutes per session, and build towards two to four sessions a week if you can. Hydrate before and after, skip it entirely if you have been drinking, and come out if you feel lightheaded rather than chasing a time on the clock. If you have heart disease or low blood pressure, or you are pregnant, clear it with your doctor first.
