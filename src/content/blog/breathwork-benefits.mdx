Breathwork is the strangest of the five practices, because two very different methods both get called by the same word. One calms you down, the other winds you up, and confusing them is how people get it wrong. Here is the honest read on both.

## Slow breathing: the calming case

Box breathing, and slow breathing generally, is the well-grounded end of breathwork. When you slow your breath to around six breaths a minute and lengthen the exhale, you shift the balance of your nervous system towards the parasympathetic, rest-and-digest side. Heart rate variability rises, and people reliably report lower stress and anxiety in the moment. The evidence for acute calming is solid. It is a genuinely useful, zero-risk tool for a stressful day, before sleep, or before something you are nervous about.

## The Wim Hof method: the surprising case

The stimulating end of breathwork is high-ventilation breathing followed by breath holds, popularised by Wim Hof and usually paired with cold. The landmark evidence is a 2014 study in which people trained in the method were injected with a bacterial toxin. The trained group produced more adrenaline, released more of the anti-inflammatory signal IL-10, showed fewer inflammatory markers, and reported milder flu-like symptoms than untrained controls ([Kox et al., 2014](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1322174111)). That was striking, because voluntarily influencing the innate immune response was thought impossible.

I want to be careful here. It was a small study in healthy young men, it bundled breathing with cold exposure and mindset, and a later systematic review found the wider health claims are under-studied ([PLOS ONE, 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0286933)). So the honest position is: something real happens acutely, but it does not prove the method treats or prevents any disease.

## The autophagy angle

The version this app leans on is intermittent hypoxic breathing, the breath-hold side of the practice, which briefly and repeatedly lowers oxygen. Mild, repeated hypoxia is a hormetic stressor, and there is mechanistic reason to think it engages the cellular clean-up processes, including autophagy, that underpin the whole approach. This is the least studied of the five practices in humans, so I hold it more lightly than fasting, exercise or sauna. It is cheap to test and worth including, but do not overclaim it.

## How to do it safely

The single most important rule: heavy breathing methods can make you faint. Never do Wim Hof style breathing in or near water, in the bath, while driving, or standing up. Sit or lie down somewhere safe. Slow box breathing carries essentially no risk and can be done anywhere. Start with a few minutes of slow breathing to settle, keep the intense methods short and seated, and stop if you feel unwell.
